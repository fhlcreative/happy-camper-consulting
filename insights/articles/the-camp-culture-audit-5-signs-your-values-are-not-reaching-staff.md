How to tell when your culture is admired in theory but inconsistent in practice

By Jeff Rorabaugh, Founder & Principal Consultant

### **Introduction**

Most camps can name their values.

Hospitality.

Safety.

Joy.

Ownership.

Teamwork.

Faithfulness.

Those words often appear in handbooks, staff training, board decks, donor language, and website copy.

But the real question is not whether a camp can state its values.

The real question is whether staff can live them consistently.

A healthy camp culture is not proven by the language leaders admire.

It is proven by the behaviors staff repeat under pressure.

That is why every camp needs a culture audit from time to time.

Not a branding exercise.

Not a morale speech.

A real audit.

One that asks whether your values are actually reaching the places that matter most: cabins, supervision, correction, reporting, communication, and daily staff behavior.

### **Why values often fail to travel**

Most camps do not struggle because their values are wrong.

They struggle because their values remain abstract.

Leaders say good things.

Staff nod in agreement.

Training week feels strong.

But once camp gets busy, values that were never translated begin to thin out.

This is where culture drift begins.

Not because the mission changed.

Because the mission was not reinforced clearly enough in practice.

As we often say, a behavior is a value in action (/insights/a-behavior-is-a-value-in-action).

If the behavior is inconsistent, the value has not yet been translated well enough.

### **5 signs your values are not reaching staff**

If any of these feel familiar, your camp may have stronger language than translation.

##### **1. Staff can repeat the values, but not apply them**

This is one of the clearest signs.

If you ask staff what your camp values are, they may answer correctly.

But if you ask what those values require during a real moment of tension, the answers get fuzzy.

For example:

- What does hospitality look like when a camper is dysregulated?
- What does ownership look like when a counselor makes a mistake?
- What does teamwork require when another staff member is dropping the ball?
- What does safety mean when something feels off but is not yet a formal incident?

If staff know the words but not the behaviors, the culture is still too conceptual.

Values must be translated into actions people can recognize, practice, and repeat.

##### **2. Different supervisors reinforce different standards**

When culture is healthy, staff should not experience one set of expectations in one area of camp and a different set somewhere else.

But many camps quietly operate this way.

One supervisor addresses tone directly.

Another ignores it.

One insists on early reporting.

Another minimizes small concerns.

One leader follows through carefully.

Another leads mostly by instinct.

This creates a hidden but serious problem.

It teaches staff that values are flexible depending on who is in charge.

That does not create healthy culture.

It creates confusion.

This is why healthy camp leadership teams (/insights/what-healthy-camp-leadership-teams-do-differently) matter so much. Alignment at the leadership level is what makes values feel consistent at the staff level.

##### **3. Correction is inconsistent or delayed**

Culture is not only built through inspiration.

It is also built through correction.

If a camp says it values respect, but sharp tone goes unaddressed, staff learn something important.

If a camp says it values accountability, but avoidable patterns are tolerated too long, staff learn something important.

If a camp says it values communication, but reporting delays are rarely challenged, staff learn something important.

They learn what leadership actually protects.

That is why camps drift when supervisors avoid hard conversations.

Delayed correction makes values feel optional.

Strong culture requires leaders who are willing to name small issues early, calmly, and consistently.

##### **4. Staff performance depends too much on personality**

If your strongest cultural consistency depends on a few unusually mature people, your culture is probably thinner than it appears.

In some camps, excellent returning staff carry the tone, fill relational gaps, and model behaviors that the broader system has not clearly embedded.

That can make the camp look healthier than it really is.

But if the culture weakens significantly when a few key people are gone, the values are not yet durable.

Healthy camps do not depend only on charismatic transmitters.

They build repeatable systems that make the values visible across cabins, teams, and summers.

That is part of how healthy camps strengthen before strain.

##### **5. Leaders talk about culture more than they inspect it**

Many camps are comfortable celebrating culture.

Far fewer are disciplined about inspecting it.

That inspection should sound like questions such as:

- Where are our values showing up clearly right now?
- Where are they breaking down?
- Which behaviors are being rewarded in practice?
- What are staff experiencing differently from what leaders intend?
- Where does confusion keep repeating?

If leaders do not inspect culture, they usually overestimate it.

Not because they are dishonest.

Because busyness hides inconsistency.

Culture audits help leaders see what routine can blur.

### **What a simple culture audit can include**

A culture audit does not need to be complicated to be useful.

Start by reviewing four things:

##### **1. Repeated behavior patterns**

Where are the same issues surfacing again and again?

Tone? Follow-through? Reporting delays? Cabin inconsistency? Supervisor passivity?

##### **2. Leadership alignment**

Are supervisors reinforcing the same standards in the same way?

Or does staff experience leadership differently depending on who is on duty?

##### **3. Translation clarity**

Can staff explain what your values look like in real moments?

Not just in theory. In practice.

##### **4. Feedback and correction rhythms**

Are leaders addressing issues while they are still small?

Or does most correction happen late, inconsistently, or only after a larger disruption?

These questions help reveal whether your culture is lived or merely described.

### **How healthy camps close the gap**

If you discover that your values are not reaching staff clearly, the answer is not shame.

It is structure.

Healthy camps close the gap by doing a few things well:

1. Translate each value into observable behavior. Do not stop at ideals. Define what the value looks like in cabins, supervision, communication, and correction.
2. Train supervisors to reinforce values consistently. Culture drifts quickly when leadership language is uneven.
3. Use real scenarios. Staff learn culture faster when they rehearse real moments, not just hear inspirational language.
4. Correct early. Values become believable when leaders protect them in small moments.
5. Review patterns regularly. Healthy culture is reinforced through attention, not assumption.

Healthy Culture. Clear Systems. Sustainable Camps.

That is not just branding language.

It is the work of translation, reinforcement, and follow-through.

## **Conclusion**

A camp’s stated values may be beautiful.

But if staff cannot apply them clearly, they are not yet strong enough.

Culture is not what your camp says on its best day.

Culture is what your people repeatedly do on ordinary days under real pressure.

That is why culture audits matter.

They help leaders see whether the mission is actually reaching behavior.

When values are translated clearly, reinforced consistently, and protected through leadership action, camps become better equipped.

Staff lead with more confidence.

Supervisors correct with more clarity.

Directors carry less uncertainty.

And the culture becomes more durable across the whole operation.

Healthy camps don't happen by accident. They are built — on purpose.
